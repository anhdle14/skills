# Research writing reference

Use when writing a research paper, proposal, literature review, or related-work section.

1. Define the field, research question, audience, and claim before searching. Search the field's primary literature and authoritative datasets or standards; use arXiv for relevant preprints, not as a substitute for field-specific venues.
2. Verify each source from its landing page or full text: title, authors, persistent identifier, version, publication venue/status, and the specific claim it supports. Record sources and notes in a durable file for substantial work.
3. Follow references that materially support the argument and inspect later citing work when available. Search across adjacent terminology and report the search scope; do not imply exhaustive coverage from a few results.
4. Distinguish preprints from peer-reviewed publications, versions from one another, and results from interpretations. Check for corrections, retractions, limitations, and conflicting findings where practical.
5. Cite close to the supported claim. Cross-reference related work by explaining its relationship to the current argument, not by listing papers. Do not claim novelty without an appropriately scoped search.
6. Never fabricate a citation, identifier, quotation, result, or consensus. Mark unresolved metadata or claims as unverified and ask for access when full text cannot be checked.

For arXiv version behavior and identifiers, consult [arXiv versioning guidance](https://info.arxiv.org/help/versions.html). Use the relevant field's own databases and publication norms as additional sources.
